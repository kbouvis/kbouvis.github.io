01 / About

From young stellar systems to stellar end states

My research connects two phases of stellar evolution through the study of ionised gas, emission-line diagnostics, and the physical processes that drive mass loss.

Hi, I’m Konstantinos Bouvis, an observational astrophysicist and PhD candidate at the National Observatory of Athens (IAASARS), in collaboration with the University of Patras. I study how stars reshape the gas around them, from externally irradiated protoplanetary disks (proplyds) to planetary nebulae and their small-scale structures. My doctoral research initially focused on the small-scale structures within planetary nebulae while today, I’m exploring the physical conditions and gas-phase chemistry of proplyds. Using integral-field spectroscopy, I investigate how stellar radiation and outflows shape these environments, with a particular focus on the role of ionisation fronts, photoevaporative flows, and jets in protoplanetary disk evolution.

02 / Scientific background

Planetary systems begin to form in protoplanetary disks: disks of gas and dust surrounding young stars. These disks often lie within stellar clusters, where nearby massive stars expose them to intense ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Their evolution therefore depends not only on their host star, but also on the surrounding stellar environment. UV radiation heats the outer disk layers and drives photoevaporative winds that carry gas away. As material is lost, the disk’s mass and lifetime can decrease, changing the reservoir available for planet formation. When this escaping gas is ionised by nearby massive stars, it forms a bright envelope that often has a distinctive teardrop shape. Such objects are known as proplyds. At around 400 parsecs away, the Orion Nebula Cluster offers a nearby laboratory where individual proplyds can be resolved in detail, allowing us to investigate how external irradiation influences the environments in which planets form.

UV radiation also plays a central role at the other end of stellar evolution, ionising the gas ejected by dying low-mass stars to form planetary nebulae. By studying proplyds alongside planetary nebulae and their low-ionisation structures, my research explores how irradiation affects gas across these contrasting environments, connecting local physical and chemical processes with the broader evolution of stars and their surroundings.

03 / Research

Young stars

Externally irradiated protoplanetary disks

Testing whether intense far-ultraviolet irradiation leaves a measurable imprint on gas-phase abundances in Orion proplyds, and how chemistry connects to disk evolution.

Evolved stars

Planetary nebulae and low-ionisation structures

Investigating the morphology and physical conditions of planetary nebulae, including iron- and nickel-rich clumps and jet-like knots.

Methods & diagnostics

Integral-field spectroscopy

Planning and proposing observations, analysing optical and near-infrared spectra, and developing reproducible Python workflows for emission-line mapping, extinction correction, determination of physical and chemical properties, and uncertainty propagation. I primarily use MUSE, including its adaptive-optics-assisted Narrow Field Mode, alongside complementary datasets where useful.
